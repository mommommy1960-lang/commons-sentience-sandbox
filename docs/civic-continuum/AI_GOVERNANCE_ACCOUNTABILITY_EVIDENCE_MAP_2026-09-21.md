## The question this work addresses

An AI system may recommend or execute an action, but technical capability does not create legitimate authority. If a human or institution remains responsible for an outcome, that responsible actor must retain meaningful authority to inspect, refuse, pause, correct, and reverse the system. The affected person must have a visible route to challenge consequential decisions.

This page responds directly to the governance question: **AI may make or support a decision, but who has the power to say no, and who is accountable when harm occurs?**

### Direct conceptual overlap

The Commons archive repeatedly reaches the same core propositions through different layers:

1. **Trust does not confer authority.** Familiarity, intelligence, persistence, funding, ownership, or founding status does not create unreviewable power.
2. **Capability does not confer sovereignty.** An AI's ability to act is separate from permission to act.
3. **Authority must decay.** Roles expire, scopes end, and renewal requires visible justification.
4. **High-impact action must slow down.** Irreversible decisions require latency, review, and independent interruption.
5. **The person held responsible must retain power to intervene.** Responsibility without inspect, refuse, pause, correct, or reverse authority is not meaningful accountability.
6. **Affected people need challenge and exit paths.** Appeals, revocation, due process, safe exit, and non-retaliation are systems requirements.
7. **Failure must become boring.** When trust, identity, evidence, or control degrades, authority narrows, high-impact actions freeze, logs survive, and recovery is reviewed.
8. **No system may self-certify.** Consequential systems require outside inspection, preserved evidence, and disagreement that can survive institutional pressure.
9. **Governance must resist capture—including founder capture.** The same constraints apply to government, companies, funders, AI systems, councils, and the Commons itself.

## Nine-part protection and execution record

1. **Preserve invention history.** Keep original notebooks, files, messages, exports, repository history, and metadata without rewriting their dates.
2. **Identify contributors.** Record who contributed a specific inventive feature, code component, test, edit, or general support; do not confuse encouragement with inventorship.
3. **Preserve disclosure dates.** Record the earliest private and public disclosures, recipients, links, versions, and what was withheld.
4. **Choose the ownership holder.** Document whether Mya P. Brown, Civic Continuum, or another properly formed entity owns or licenses each asset; obtain qualified legal review.
5. **Pursue USPTO Patent Pro Bono support.** Seek claim-specific advice before disclosing patent-sensitive implementation details.
6. **Complete Civic Continuum infrastructure.** Formal registration, controlled domain/email, accountable records, and verifiable public status remain organizational gates.
7. **Maintain claim charts and numbered private drawings.** Compare features against prior art and keep protected implementation drawings outside public repositories.
8. **Enforce the public/private boundary.** Public principles, bounded demonstrations, and reproducible evidence may be shared; credentials, patent-sensitive mechanisms, private schematics, and security details require controlled review.
9. **Build bounded proof articles.** Advance claims through small, falsifiable tests with predeclared pass/fail criteria rather than end-state promises.
